The Most Important Skill I Didn't Expect to Learn in a SOC

Yasir Alharbi Security Operations Center Communication
Contents

When people think about a Security Operations Center (SOC), they usually think about technical skills.

SIEM platforms.

Threat intelligence.

Windows Event Logs.

Malware.

Detection engineering.

These are all essential.

But after working in a SOC, I discovered that one of the most valuable skills wasn't technical at all.

It was communication.

An Investigation Has No Value If Nobody Understands It

Imagine spending an hour investigating a suspicious alert.

You review logs, correlate events, verify timelines, and confidently determine that the activity is benign.

Now imagine documenting your findings in a way that nobody else understands.

The investigation may be technically correct, but it has failed its purpose.

Your manager can't review it.

The next analyst can't continue it.

The incident response team can't act on it.

Good analysis deserves good communication.

Technical Doesn't Mean Complicated

Early in my career, I thought writing technical reports meant using complex language.

I eventually realized the opposite is true.

The best analysts explain technical concepts clearly.

Instead of trying to impress readers with terminology, they focus on answering simple questions:

  • What happened?
  • Why is it important?
  • What evidence supports the conclusion?
  • What should happen next?

Clear communication builds confidence.

Complicated communication often creates confusion.

Different Audiences Need Different Answers

One investigation may be read by several people.

A SOC analyst wants technical details.

An incident responder wants evidence.

A detection engineer wants to know whether the rule should be improved.

A manager wants to understand business impact.

Although everyone is reading the same investigation, they are looking for different information.

Learning to communicate with different audiences is just as important as learning to investigate.

Documentation Is Part of the Investigation

Many analysts think documentation begins after the investigation ends.

I see it differently.

Good documentation is part of the investigation itself.

Writing forces you to organize your thoughts.

It highlights missing evidence.

It exposes assumptions.

If you struggle to explain why you reached a conclusion, you may need to investigate further.

In many cases, writing improves the quality of the investigation.

A Good Report Saves Someone Else Time

SOC work rarely happens in isolation.

Investigations are handed over between shifts.

Cases are escalated.

Months later, the same activity may appear again.

A well-written investigation allows another analyst to understand what happened without starting from scratch.

That's not just good documentation.

It's good teamwork.

Communication Builds Trust

Technical skills help analysts find answers.

Communication helps others trust those answers.

When investigations are clear, concise, and supported by evidence, stakeholders become more confident in the SOC's decisions.

Over time, that trust becomes one of the team's greatest strengths.

Final Thoughts

Cybersecurity will continue to evolve.

New attack techniques will emerge.

New tools will be released.

New detection methods will be developed.

But one skill will always remain valuable:

The ability to explain complex technical problems clearly, accurately, and confidently.

Finding the answer is important.

Helping others understand it is what turns good analysts into trusted professionals.
