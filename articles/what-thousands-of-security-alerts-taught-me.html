What Thousands of Security Alerts Taught Me That No Certification Ever Could

Yasir Alharbi Security Operations Center Analyst reflection
Contents

When I started my career in cybersecurity, I believed that becoming a better analyst meant earning more certifications, learning more tools, and memorizing more attack techniques.

Those things certainly matter. Certifications provide a solid foundation, and technical knowledge is essential. But after spending countless hours investigating security alerts in a Security Operations Center (SOC), I realized that the biggest lessons weren't found in books or exams—they came from experience.

A SIEM Doesn't Detect Attacks—It Detects Conditions

One of the first misconceptions I had was believing that every alert represented malicious activity.

In reality, a SIEM doesn't know whether something is an attack. It only knows that a predefined rule has been triggered.

The analyst's job begins where the SIEM stops.

Every alert requires context:

  • Is this behavior expected?
  • Has it happened before?
  • Is the user authorized to perform this action?
  • Is this system behaving differently than usual?
  • Does the timeline support malicious activity?

Without answering these questions, an alert is simply data—not evidence.

The Biggest Enemy Isn't Attackers

Before working in a SOC, I assumed attackers would be my biggest challenge. Instead, I discovered that noise is often the real adversary.

Analysts spend much of their time separating meaningful events from expected business activity. False positives, repetitive alerts, and incomplete logging can easily distract investigators from incidents that truly matter.

This experience taught me that effective detection isn't about generating more alerts—it's about generating better ones.

Curiosity Is More Valuable Than Assumptions

Over time, I noticed a pattern. The best investigations didn't begin with someone saying, "This looks malicious." They began with someone asking, "Can I prove this is normal?"

That small shift in mindset changes everything.

Good analysts collect evidence before reaching conclusions. They validate assumptions instead of relying on intuition. In cybersecurity, confidence should come from evidence, not from experience alone.

Digital Forensics Changed My Perspective

As I studied Digital Forensics and Incident Response (DFIR), I began approaching alerts differently. Instead of viewing an alert as the end of an investigation, I started treating it as the beginning.

A single detection can lead to process execution artifacts, Windows Event Logs, registry changes, scheduled tasks, browser history, memory artifacts, or file system evidence.

The SIEM tells you where to look. Forensics tells you what actually happened.

Understanding both disciplines creates stronger investigations than relying on either one alone.

Looking Ahead

Today, artificial intelligence is rapidly becoming part of cybersecurity workflows. AI can summarize logs, correlate events, and accelerate investigations. These capabilities will undoubtedly improve SOC operations.

However, AI still depends on human judgment.

It cannot replace an analyst's understanding of business context, investigative reasoning, or the ability to distinguish between suspicious and malicious activity.

Rather than replacing analysts, I believe AI will amplify those who already understand the fundamentals.

Final Thoughts

If there's one lesson my SOC experience has reinforced, it's this:

Cybersecurity isn't about memorizing commands or collecting certifications. It's about learning how to think.

The tools will change. Attack techniques will evolve. Detection rules will be rewritten.

But the ability to ask the right questions, gather evidence objectively, and understand context will remain one of the most valuable skills any analyst can develop.

That's the lesson no certification alone can teach—and one I continue to learn every day.
