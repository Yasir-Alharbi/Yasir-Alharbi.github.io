Why Context Is the Most Powerful Detection Rule

Yasir Alharbi Security Operations Center Detection thinking

Security Operations Centers generate thousands of alerts every day. Many of them look suspicious. Only a few are actually malicious.

The difference between the two is rarely determined by the detection rule itself. It's determined by context.

The Same Event Can Mean Two Completely Different Things

Imagine a detection rule triggers because PowerShell was executed. Should you escalate the alert? The answer is: it depends.

PowerShell launched by a system administrator during scheduled maintenance is completely different from PowerShell executed by Microsoft Word after a user opened an email attachment.

The event is the same. The context is not.

Without context, both alerts appear equally suspicious.

Detection Rules Are Only the Starting Point

One lesson I learned while working in a SOC is that detection rules don't make decisions. They raise questions.

A good alert should encourage an analyst to investigate, not immediately conclude that an attack has occurred. Questions such as these often matter more than the alert itself:

  • Who executed the process?
  • Which host generated the event?
  • Has this behavior been seen before?
  • What happened immediately before and after the alert?
  • Is this expected within the organization's environment?

Answering these questions transforms raw telemetry into an informed investigation.

Looking Beyond a Single Log

One log rarely tells the full story. Suppose an endpoint reports multiple failed logon attempts. On its own, this could indicate password spraying. Or it could simply be a user typing the wrong password after returning from vacation.

Now add more context:

  • The source IP belongs to an internal management server.
  • The account successfully authenticated seconds later.
  • Similar activity occurs every morning at the same time.

Suddenly, what looked suspicious becomes understandable. The investigation changed—not because of a different detection rule, but because additional evidence was considered.

Context Reduces Alert Fatigue

Alert fatigue is one of the biggest challenges facing SOC teams. The solution isn't always writing more rules. Sometimes it's improving the quality of the information surrounding existing detections.

Asset criticality, user roles, authentication history, known administrative activity, maintenance windows, and historical behavior all provide valuable context.

When analysts have this information available, they spend less time chasing noise and more time focusing on genuine threats.

Technology Helps—But Judgment Matters

Modern security platforms are becoming increasingly intelligent. Machine learning models can identify anomalies. AI can summarize investigations and correlate related events. Automation can enrich alerts within seconds.

These capabilities improve efficiency, but they don't replace human reasoning. A system may identify something as unusual. An analyst determines whether it matters.

That distinction remains essential.

Final Thoughts

Detection engineering is often viewed as the process of writing better rules. I believe it's equally about providing better context.

A simple detection supported by rich context is often more valuable than a complex detection that leaves analysts guessing.

The next time an alert appears in your queue, don't ask only, "What rule fired?"

Ask a better question: "What is the complete story behind this event?"

More often than not, that's where the real investigation begins.
